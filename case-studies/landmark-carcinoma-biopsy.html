Diagnosing Carcinoma: Why the Biopsy Is the Gold Standard

How looking directly at cells and tissue — the foundation of modern pathology — became the definitive way to diagnose cancer.

For most cancers, the diagnosis is confirmed the same way — a pathologist examines the tissue itself.

What happened

In 1858, Dr Rudolf Virchow — often called the father of cellular pathology — argued that disease begins in cells (“omnis cellula e cellula”, every cell from a cell). That idea put the microscope at the centre of diagnosis. A carcinoma is a cancer that arises from epithelial cells — the cells that line and cover the body’s surfaces and glands. Confirming one means examining a biopsy or resection under the microscope to see how the cells and tissue are organised.

Why it mattered

Imaging and blood tests can raise suspicion, but the biopsy remains the definitive answer. It shows whether a growth is benign or malignant, what type it is, and often how aggressive it looks (its “grade”). That diagnosis shapes the entire treatment plan. On difficult cases, an expert second-opinion review adds another layer of confidence before major decisions are made.

Key points

  • A carcinoma is a cancer that arises from epithelial cells
  • Histopathology of a biopsy or resection confirms the diagnosis and the tumour type
  • Grading describes how abnormal the cells look and helps guide treatment
  • Expert review / second opinion supports confident reporting on difficult cases
