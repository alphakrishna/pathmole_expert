HIV: from Discovery to Molecular Testing

How isolating a virus and building tests to detect it turned a once-fatal infection into a manageable, monitored condition.

Identifying the virus was step one; molecular testing is what made early detection and treatment monitoring possible.

What happened

In 1983, Dr Françoise Barré-Sinoussi and Dr Luc Montagnier at the Institut Pasteur isolated the human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) and established it as the cause of AIDS — work recognised with the 2008 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine. That discovery opened the door to blood-screening tests and, later, to molecular tests that detect the virus itself.

Why it mattered

Molecular (nucleic-acid) testing can detect HIV earlier than antibody tests and can measure how much virus is present — the “viral load.” That made it possible to start treatment sooner, track how well it is working, and reduce the risk of transmission. With effective antiretroviral therapy, HIV has become a manageable long-term condition rather than a certain death sentence.

Key points

  • HIV was isolated in 1983 and shown to cause AIDS (Nobel Prize, 2008)
  • Molecular testing can detect the virus earlier than antibody-based tests
  • Viral-load measurement helps guide and monitor treatment
  • Early detection and therapy transformed HIV into a manageable condition
